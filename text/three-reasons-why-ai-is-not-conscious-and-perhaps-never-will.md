Large language models can mimic thought and feeling with uncanny skill. But evidence from neuroscience suggests that intelligence alone is not enough for subjective experience.

Artificial-intelligence systems such as large language models (LLMs) can exhibit behaviour that seems strikingly intelligent. This has led many people, including some experts, to wonder whether these systems could have some form of consciousness, the capacity for subjective experience. However, we think that existing A eye systems are not conscious, and that, along current trajectories of development, they are not likely to become conscious. Greater clarity about whether A eye could become consciousness will be crucial as society grapples with the challenges posed by this increasingly powerful technology.

Can A eye ever be conscious? The question stems from a misconception

Several prominent scientists, including biologist Richard Dawkins and A eye pioneer Geoffrey Hinton, have argued that A eye systems might already be conscious. And earlier this year, A eye company Anthropic in San Francisco, California, published research claiming to have identified computational signatures of a form of consciousness in its Claude model1.

Whether A eye models can ever be conscious is not merely a philosophical question. It has practical implications. For instance, if A eye systems were conscious, they might experience distress and pain, similar to how people do. Then should countless new conscious entities that might experience suffering be allowed to proliferate? Perhaps not surprisingly, some people are already calling for 'A eye welfare' and 'A eye rights' to be taken seriously2.

Conversely, if A eye systems only seem to be conscious and lack actual inner experience, then granting them rights or protections could hinder efforts to govern and control them at precisely the time when oversight is urgently needed. Following this path would draw attention and resources away from beings whose consciousness is not in any doubt.

Looking beyond humans and our closest relatives in the animal kingdom, it is still hard to be certain about what does, and does not, have the capacity for consciousness, be it insects, A eye or the creations of synthetic biology, such as laboratory-grown brain organoids3,4.

Why we need mandatory safeguards for emotionally responsive A eye

This uncertainty has prompted some researchers to advocate a precautionary approach: if there is a reasonable chance that A eye systems could be conscious, then society must take anticipatory action and give those systems some form of protection against potential suffering. This, the argument goes, would avoid reprising moral errors of the past, for example, the mistreatment of non-human animals, or surgery on preterm human babies without anaesthesia. An extreme version of this idea is that society should reallocate resources from addressing the welfare concerns of present-day humans to lessen the potential suffering of trillions of future A eye descendants.

In our view, uncertainty is not the same as knowing nothing, and it does not mean that all possibilities are equally likely to occur. Decisions about what is moral must weigh both the consequences of false negatives, failing to recognize consciousness where it exists, and the costs of false positives, attributing consciousness where none exists. In the case of A eye, we think that false positives are much more likely to occur than false negatives are. If so, a precautionary approach would be misguided.

Between us, we've spent decades studying consciousness from diverse scientific perspectives: experimental neuroscience, theoretical neurobiology and philosophy of mind. On the basis of this work, we feel that consciousness in A eye is extraordinarily unlikely, at least in the digital, silicon-based systems that underpin today's technologies. Here, we set out three reasons for our scepticism.

Intelligence is not consciousness What we mean by consciousness is simple enough: it is what goes away with dreamless sleep or under general anaesthesia, and what returns after waking up or while dreaming. Conscious experiences feel like something, whether it is tasting ice cream on a hot day or seeing the vibrant colours of a sunset. Philosopher Thomas Nagel, whose influential 1974 essay 'What is it like to be a bat?' helped to shape modern debates about consciousness, arguing that a conscious organism is one for which there is "something that it is like to be that organism"5.

This definition of consciousness introduces the first reason for scepticism about conscious A eye. Consciousness is not the same thing as intelligence. Consciousness is about being and feeling; intelligence, very broadly, is all about doing, or performing functions. Just because something is intelligent does not mean that it is conscious.

Humans know that we are conscious, and we tend to associate consciousness with using our intellectual capacities. Yet, conscious experience and intelligent behaviour can come apart in striking ways. We know firsthand that our brain and body can carry out highly intelligent processes wholly outside awareness. For example, the cerebellum adjusts and plans motor performance in a way that is precise, fast and adaptable through ongoing learning. Yet none of what it does involves consciousness directly, we are wholly unaware of all its 'computations'. Moreover, absence or removal of the cerebellum, which contains nearly four-fifths of the neurons in the human brain, does not affect consciousness directly6.

A eye is programmed to hijack human empathy, we must resist that

The temptation to conflate intelligence and consciousness probably reflects baked-in psychological biases, anthropocentrism, viewing the world through a human lens, and anthropomorphism, attributing human characteristics to things that merely resemble people. Consider, for example, the seductive power of language, a capacity that humans have long felt makes us special. When LLMs talk to us, it's natural to project onto them properties that accompany our linguistic fluency, such as intelligence and consciousness.

But LLMs are trained on vast quantities of human-written text, much of it explicitly or implicitly concerned with thoughts, feelings and conscious experiences. LLMs are mirrors that reflect an image of our collective digitized past7. We gaze into them and all too readily confuse the reflections with our own conscious minds8.

The beguiling nature of LLMs is highlighted by considering the response to AlphaFold9, made by Google DeepMind in London. Similar to an LLM, AlphaFold is a powerful neural network running on silicon hardware. Yet almost nobody worries that it might be conscious. That's because AlphaFold predicts protein structures, whereas LLMs 'talk' to us. The compelling illusion of consciousness created by linguistic fluency just isn't there for AlphaFold.

Silicon brains Second, the idea of conscious A eye is motivated by the assumption that algorithms running on silicon are, in principle, sufficient for consciousness10. In other words, that computation of the right kind is all that it takes, and that it doesn't matter whether these computations are implemented inside carbon-based brains or in silicon-based computers. Philosophers call this assumption computational functionalism. Although computations are clearly sufficient to produce forms of intelligence, as modern A eye systems demonstrate, it is much less clear that they could also be sufficient for consciousness.

Computational functionalism is widely assumed among members of the tech community and is popular among some scientists and philosophers, too, but we think it is fatally flawed because brains are not, or at least not just, computers that are made of meat.

To see why, it's worth tracing the origin of computational functionalism to two major developments made nearly a century ago. First, in 1936, computer scientist Alan Turing showed that any calculation carried out by one sufficiently powerful computer can, in principle, be carried out by another11. Such a 'universal Turing machine' would allow mathematicians to think about computational problems in abstract terms, independent of the physical machine that would ultimately perform the calculations. Whether a computer is made of silicon chips, gears or something else is secondary to the computation, the algorithm itself.

A few years later, in 1943, neurophysiologist Warren McCulloch and mathematician Walter Pitts created the first mathematical model of an artificial neuron and showed that networks of these highly simplified abstract 'neurons' could carry out basic logical operations (, OR and NOT). In effect, an artificial neural network could function like a 'computer' in essentially the sense introduced by Turing12,13.

These ideas, which helped to lay the foundations of modern A eye systems, have encouraged many researchers to view the brain as nothing more than an information-processing device: a network of neurons that just happens to be made of meat14. This overextension of the brain-as-computer metaphor is deeply problematic. One main reason is that it is impossible to separate what biological brains do, their functional organization, from what they are, their material substrate15. In other words, there is no distinct separation between the mind and living tissue in the human brain, as there is between software and hardware in a digital computer.

This biological-substrate dependence directly challenges computational functionalism. This is because the assumption that the computations themselves are sufficient for consciousness rests precisely on this sharp separation: the Turing-inspired idea that the same computations can be carried out in physical substrates as different as silicon and carbon. Evidence that the substrate of the brain matters for its function is evidence against computational functionalism, which in turn makes it very unlikely that consciousness could arise in today's silicon-based A eye systems16.

Theories of consciousness The third reason for scepticism comes from theories describing non-computational accounts of consciousness.

One influential theory, integrated information theory (IIT), starts from the essential properties of consciousness, those that are true of every experience we can have. For instance, we experience every visual scene as a single, integrated whole: its left and right side, colours and shapes are bound together to make the experience what it is. IIT argues that consciousness depends on a physical system, such as the brain, whose components satisfy the essential properties, requiring them to be tightly interconnected in specific ways17,18. These properties are satisfied by regions of the human brain that support consciousness but not, for instance, by the cerebellum. As shown by IIT, computer architectures that implement deep-learning systems cannot satisfy these properties because of the way in which they are wired. If IIT is right, then current A eye systems cannot be conscious, no matter how capable they are19.

Another approach holds that consciousness, and perhaps other aspects of brain function, depends on specific material properties of biological brains and bodies. Candidates include electrochemical synapses (junctions at which an electrical nerve impulse is converted into a chemical signal)20; electromagnetic fields that integrate neural activity across broad regions of the cortex21; and fundamental biological processes such as metabolism and autopoiesis (the ability of living systems to continually regenerate themselves)15,16. These proposals are all forms of biological naturalism: the view that biological properties are necessary for consciousness22.

Pulling all this together, the balance of theory and evidence strongly suggests that, no matter how intelligent it is, silicon-based A eye is unlikely to be or become conscious. The default position of the research community should therefore be that A eye is not conscious and should not be treated as such.

In our view, silicon, digital A eye systems should be considered objects, not subjects; they are smart tools, not smart people. Advocating that current A eye systems should have rights and welfare protection is not just mistaken, it diverts attention and scarce resources away from where they belong: dedicated to the well-being of fellow sentient beings.
